Night log

The hill behind the reservoir is dark enough for a small glass. I set
the tripod where the path flattens and let the mirror cool while the
thermos was still too hot to hold.

Vega was the first star I could name without the chart. It sits high
on summer evenings and does not twinkle as hard as the ones near the
horizon. The chart calls that steady look good seeing. From this hill
it mostly means the wind has dropped.

Jupiter was a disk, not a point, once the eyepiece settled. Two moons
stood off to one side like punctuation. I wrote their places down and
checked them an hour later. They had moved. That is the whole of the
observation, and it was enough to walk back happy.

The reservoir throws light from the town back up, so the southern sky
is a pale bowl. The northern sky still goes black. Cassiopeia hangs
over the water tower like a bent chair. I use it to find the faint
smear of the double cluster, which on this night was a grainy patch
and not the picture in the book.

A satellite crossed during the second look at Jupiter. It was faster
than a high plane and did not blink. I lost the moons while I watched
it and had to start the sketch over.

Close to midnight the air went soft and the brighter stars grew spikes.
That is the moment to stop. Packing the tripod in the dark is slower
than setting it up, because every clamp feels the same.

The last line of the log is the one I come back to: perigee is not a
thing you see. It is the word for the moon being as near as it gets,
and on this night the moon was elsewhere, which is why the reservoir
stayed dark enough to work.
